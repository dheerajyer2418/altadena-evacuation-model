Please read this first. This is a “what-if” experiment, not a replay of the real January-2025 Eaton Fire, and not a prediction of what would really happen. The fire here is made up, and the traffic part hasn’t been checked against real traffic data. So the numbers are only useful for comparing the strategies to each other (since every strategy is tested on the exact same setup) — not as real forecasts. The computer code has been tested for bugs; the real-world accuracy has not.

What am I looking at?

It’s a simulation (basically a detailed “SimCity”-style model) of people trying to drive out of Altadena, California during a wildfire. The big question: if self-driving cars helped with the evacuation, how should they decide who to pick up — and would that actually help the people who don’t own a car? Everybody in town is represented as a little dot that has to get to safety before the fire reaches them.

How did I build a fake town that still feels real?

SinceI can’t get a list of every real resident, I build a synthetic population instead. I take real anonymous example households and copy/arrange them until the crowd matches the official neighborhood totals — the right number of kids, seniors, big families, one-car homes, no-car homes, and so on, in each neighborhood.

The result: about 15,000 households / 41,000 people — and when I add it up it lands within about 1% of the real Census totals for Altadena. So the crowd is a close stand-in for the real town.
  • I double-checked the data and caught my own mistakes. Some Census tables aren’t published for small neighborhoods, so I swapped in ones that are. I also found and fixed two spots where I’d lined up the Census columns wrong — after the fix, the fake town matched reality much better.
  • One honest wrinkle: households with no car are rare, so my stand-in slightly overcounts them (about 4.5% vs. the real 3.7%). Close, but treat that exact number as “roughly right.”
  • On purpose, I did NOT use race to guess behavior, and I did not assume someone with a disability can’t drive. “Needs assistance” is tracked as its own separate thing.

How do traffic jams happen in the model?

Each road can only hold so many cars and can only let so many through per minute and when too many cars pile onto a road, a queue backs up and spills onto the roads behind it. Nothing says “everyone instantly arrives”: the gridlock builds up on its own, which is the whole point. Speed limits come from the real map where they’re listed, and a sensible default where they’re not.

The road capacities are standard textbook estimates, not tuned to Altadena (I had no local traffic data), and “safety” just means reaching a freeway exit toward the 210 / Pasadena — a modeling choice, not a real evacuation order.

The fire

The fire starts in the foothills and spreads outward, pushed to the southwest like a wind-driven Santa-Ana fire. As it reaches a road, that road closes. Importantly, the self-driving cars only get a forecast of where the fire is heading (with some error and a little advance warning).

Why the fire moves at the speed it does: I picked a speed that’s scary-fast but still leaves people a chance to get out. If I made it so fast that everyone gets trapped no matter what, there’d be nothing to compare between strategies. It’s an illustrative setting you can change, not a measured real-world fire speed.

“Exposed” here means the fire reached someone’s spot, which is a danger signal, not an injury or death. The model doesn’t predict casualties. Cars caught on a closing road aren’t deleted; they’re stuck and try to find another way out.

How do people decide when to leave?

Each household goes through realistic steps: they notice the fire, get ready, (if they need a ride) request one, then leave and drive to safety — each taking some time. The default timings (roughly 15 minutes to notice, 20 to pack up) are reasonable placeholders that can easily be changed. I also separate owning a phone from the phone network actually working.

The four self-driving-car strategies I compare

The fleet is an exact number of cars (e.g. 300). People without a car request a ride; everyone else drives themselves. All four strategies use the same basic rules so the only thing that changes is the strategy itself:

  • Pull out — stop picking people up once the fire gets close to the service area.
  • Normal — first-come, first-served, no special priority.
  • Help-the-vulnerable-first — prioritize people with no car and those who need assistance.
  • Smart (adaptive) — prioritize the vulnerable and only accept a ride if the car can realistically finish the whole trip before the fire would cut it off, re-checking as things change.
